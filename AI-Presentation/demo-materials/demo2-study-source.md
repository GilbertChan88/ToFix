## How Memory Works: Encoding, Storage, and Retrieval

Human memory is not a video recorder. It does not capture events perfectly and play them back on demand. Instead, memory is an active, reconstructive process built on three core stages: **encoding**, **storage**, and **retrieval**. Understanding these stages explains both why we remember and why we forget.

### 1. Encoding — getting information in
Encoding is the process of converting what we experience into a form the brain can keep. Information first passes through **sensory memory**, an extremely brief buffer (well under a second for vision). Whatever we pay attention to moves into **working memory**, a limited workspace that holds only a small number of items at once and for a short time. Attention is the gatekeeper: information we do not attend to is rarely encoded at all. Encoding is deeper and more durable when we connect new information to things we already know — a principle called **elaboration**. This is why understanding a concept leads to better memory than rote repetition.

### 2. Storage — keeping information over time
Information that is encoded well can be consolidated into **long-term memory**, which has an enormous capacity and can last a lifetime. **Consolidation** is the process of stabilising a memory after it is first formed, and sleep plays a major role in it. Long-term memory is not one thing: **explicit (declarative)** memory covers facts and events we can consciously state, while **implicit** memory covers skills and habits, like riding a bike, that we perform without conscious recall.

### 3. Retrieval — getting information back out
Retrieval is accessing stored information when we need it. Retrieval is strongly helped by **cues** — the smell, place, or word that triggers a memory. A key finding is that memory is better when the context at retrieval matches the context at encoding. Importantly, the *act* of retrieving strengthens a memory: this is the **testing effect**, which is why self-quizzing beats re-reading as a study method.

### Why we forget
Forgetting is not simply "losing" data. Common causes include **decay** (memory traces fade without use), **interference** (similar memories compete and blur), and **retrieval failure** (the memory is there, but the right cue is missing — the "tip of the tongue" feeling). Because memory is reconstructive, recall can also be distorted, and confident memories are not always accurate.

### Practical implications for studying
Three evidence-based strategies follow directly from this model:
1. **Spaced practice** — spreading study across days beats cramming, because it allows repeated consolidation.
2. **Retrieval practice** — testing yourself (flashcards, past questions) strengthens memory more than re-reading.
3. **Elaboration** — explaining ideas in your own words and linking them to what you already know encodes them more deeply.

In short: attention gets information in, sleep and consolidation keep it, and self-testing gets it back out reliably.

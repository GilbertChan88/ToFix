## SECTION 1 — THE EVOLUTION OF AI (≈ 4 min)

### SLIDE 4 — AI didn't start with ChatGPT
**[ON SLIDE]**
- 1950s: the idea
- 1980s–90s: rules & expert systems
- 2010s: machine learning wave
- 2022→: generative AI for everyone

**[SPEAKER NOTES]**
"AI is older than most people think. The term was coined in 1956. For decades it was 'expert systems' — humans hand-coding thousands of if-this-then-that rules. That was brittle and expensive. The 2010s brought machine learning: instead of coding rules, we let systems learn patterns from data — that's what powered spam filters, photo tagging, and recommendations. The big shift for us happened in late 2022, when generative AI — tools that *create* text, images, and audio — became something anyone could use through a simple chat box. That's the moment AI left the lab and landed on our phones."

**[TIMING]** 3:00–4:30

---

### SLIDE 5 — Why it suddenly feels everywhere
**[ON SLIDE]**
- Bigger models + more data
- Chat = no manual needed
- It now sees, hears, speaks
- Built into apps you already use

**[SPEAKER NOTES]**
"Why now? Four things came together. Models got dramatically larger and trained on far more data. The interface became plain conversation — no manual, no code. Models became 'multimodal' — they can read your PDF, look at a photo, listen to audio, and talk back. And crucially, this is now baked into Gmail, your phone keyboard, your browser. You don't 'go to' AI anymore; it comes to you. The takeaway: the skill that matters now isn't coding — it's knowing what to ask and how to check the answer."

**[TIMING]** 4:30–6:00

---

### SLIDE 6 — The one mental model to keep
**[ON SLIDE]**
- AI = a fast, confident, well-read intern
- Brilliant first drafts
- Sometimes wrong
- You stay the editor

**[SPEAKER NOTES]**
"If you remember one thing today, make it this. Treat AI like a fast, well-read intern who never sleeps. It gives you a great first draft in seconds — but it can be confidently wrong, and it doesn't know your context unless you tell it. You are always the editor and the final decision-maker. Keep that frame and you'll get the benefit without the burns. Now let's get practical."

**[TIMING]** 6:00–7:00

## SECTION 3 — DOING IT RESPONSIBLY (≈ 3 min)

### SLIDE 21 — The honest limitations
**[ON SLIDE]**
- **Hallucinations** — confident, wrong
- **Stale info** — check dates & prices
- **Bias** — reflects its training data
- **Privacy** — free ≠ private

**[SPEAKER NOTES]**
"Because this is meant to be balanced, here are the four things to genuinely watch. Hallucinations: AI can invent facts, citations, and quotes with total confidence — verify anything that matters. Stale or wrong info, especially prices and current events. Bias: it mirrors patterns in its training data, so it can carry stereotypes. And privacy: on free tiers your inputs may be used to train the model, so never paste confidential or personal data. None of these are reasons to avoid AI — they're reasons to stay the editor."

**[TIMING]** 28:00–29:30

### SLIDE 22 — Five habits of good AI users
**[ON SLIDE]**
- Give context & examples
- Ask for its reasoning
- Verify facts elsewhere
- Iterate — don't accept draft 1
- Keep the human decision

**[SPEAKER NOTES]**
"So how do the people who get great results actually use it? Five habits. One: give context and an example of what 'good' looks like — vague prompts get vague answers. Two: ask it to show its reasoning or sources. Three: verify anything factual against a second source. Four: treat the first answer as a starting point and push back — 'make it shorter,' 'more formal,' 'you missed X.' Five: keep the final judgement human. Do these and you're already ahead of most people using these tools."
